Advanced Biostatistics Analysis Laboratory

Paste biological observations, calculate the statistic, inspect assumptions, and read a defensible biological interpretation. The laboratory is designed for teaching and research training: it reports intermediate quantities, degrees of freedom, p-values, effect measures and cautions rather than presenting a bare answer.

Methodological rule: A calculator cannot repair biased sampling, pseudoreplication, confounding, non-independence or poor measurement. Choose the design and statistical model before interpreting the p-value.
Data-entry protocol: enter numbers only, without units. Commas, spaces and new lines are accepted. Missing values are never silently imputed; remove or resolve them explicitly and document the decision in a real analysis. Frequency/count tools require non-negative whole-number counts. For browser stability, each raw-data field is limited to 20,000 observations; larger research datasets should be analysed in dedicated statistical software.

1. Descriptive Statistics — Mean, Median, Mode, Variance and Standard Deviation

Use for one quantitative sample. The output includes n, sum, mean, median, mode, minimum, maximum, range, Q1, Q3, IQR, sample and population variance/SD, standard error, coefficient of variation, skewness and a 95% confidence interval for the mean.

Quantitative dataIndependent observations for inferential CICV requires a positive mean and meaningful zero
Worked sample problem

Problem: Fish lengths (cm) are 12.4, 13.1, 13.5, 14.0, 14.0, 14.7, 15.2 and 16.8. Find the mean, median, mode and sample SD.

Check: mean = 14.213 cm; median = 14.000 cm; mode = 14.0 cm; sample SD ≈ 1.364 cm. Describe centre and spread in centimetres; do not call 16.8 an outlier without a stated rule or graphical inspection.

2. Student’s t-test Laboratory

Choose one-sample, Welch independent-samples, pooled independent-samples or paired t-test. The output includes mean difference, standard error, t, degrees of freedom, two-sided p-value, 95% confidence interval and an effect-size estimate.

Worked sample problem

Problem: Six fish are weighed before and after a feed intervention. Because the same fish are measured twice, the analysis must use within-fish differences rather than treating the 12 observations as independent.

Reporting pattern: “Mean paired change = …; t(df) = …, p = …, 95% CI […, …].” Then state the biological direction and magnitude.

3. Chi-square Goodness-of-Fit

Compare observed category counts with expected counts or proportions. Leave expected blank for equal expected proportions.

Worked sample problem

Observed blood-group counts are 38, 22, 10 and 50; expected proportions are 0.30, 0.20, 0.10 and 0.40. Calculate expected frequencies, each χ² contribution, total χ², df and p.

4. Chi-square Test of Independence

Enter one contingency-table row per line. The tool computes expected frequencies, χ², df, p and Cramér’s V. For a 2×2 table it also reports a two-sided Fisher exact p-value.

Worked sample problem

Problem: Habitat (A/B) × infection (present/absent) counts are [30,20] and [15,35]. Test whether the categorical variables are independent; inspect expected frequencies before relying on χ².

5. Pearson Correlation, Spearman Correlation and Simple Linear Regression

Pearson’s r measures linear association; Spearman’s ρ measures monotonic rank association. Regression estimates the expected response Y from predictor X. Correlation does not establish causation.

Before calculation: inspect the scatterplot for non-linearity, influential outliers and restricted range.
Worked sample problem

Problem: For six fish, length X = 10,12,14,16,18,20 and weight Y = 22,29,38,49,63,78. Calculate Pearson r, fit Ŷ = a + bX, report R² and predict Y at X = 15. Interpret association without claiming that length “causes” weight.

Research enrichment

6. One-way ANOVA

Compare three or more independent group means. Enter one group per line. Reports group summaries, F, df, p and η² effect size.

Worked sample problem

Three independent diets produce three sets of larval growth measurements. ANOVA tests the omnibus null hypothesis that all population means are equal. A significant F does not identify which groups differ; post-hoc comparisons require an additional procedure.

Research enrichment

7. 2×2 Epidemiological Measures

Enter counts as exposure × outcome: a = exposed with event, b = exposed without event, c = unexposed with event, d = unexposed without event.

Guided Sample-problem Bank

These problems are deliberately biological. Students should first identify variable type, design and assumptions, then calculate, then write the conclusion in context.

Problem A — Mean, median, mode and SD

Egg counts per nest: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 8. Calculate the descriptive statistics and explain which measure of centre is most informative.

Check: mean = 5.000; median = 5.000; mode = 5; sample SD ≈ 1.512.
Problem B — Coefficient of variation

Strain A has mean mass 250 g, SD 20 g; Strain B mean 120 g, SD 15 g. Compare relative variability using CV.

Check: CV(A) = 8%; CV(B) = 12.5%; Strain B is relatively more variable.
Problem C — Paired t-test

Use the preloaded before/after fish-weight data. State why pairing is biologically and statistically necessary.

Check: mean paired increase = 2.833; t(5) ≈ 9.220; two-sided p ≈ 0.000252. Pairing is required because repeated measurements from the same fish are not independent.
Problem D — Chi-square goodness-of-fit

Use the blood-group data in Tool 3. Show the expected frequency for every category and the contribution of each cell to χ².

Check: expected counts = 36, 24, 12, 48; χ² ≈ 0.6944; df = 3; p ≈ 0.8745.
Problem E — Chi-square independence

Use the 2×2 habitat × infection table in Tool 4. Compare χ² p with Fisher exact p and explain when Fisher’s exact test becomes especially important.

Check: χ² ≈ 9.0909; df = 1; p ≈ 0.00257; Cramér’s V ≈ 0.302; two-sided Fisher exact p ≈ 0.00464.
Problem F — Correlation and regression

Use the fish length–weight pairs. Report r, p, regression equation and R². Explain why prediction outside the observed X range is extrapolation.

Check: r ≈ 0.9904; p ≈ 0.000138; Ŷ = −37.7143 + 5.6143X; R² ≈ 0.9808; predicted Y at X = 15 is 46.5.
Problem G — One-way ANOVA

Use the three groups in Tool 6. State the omnibus null hypothesis and why a post-hoc procedure is needed after a significant result.

Check: F(2,12) ≈ 22.615; p ≈ 0.0000850; η² ≈ 0.790. The omnibus result rejects equality of all means but does not specify which pairs differ.
Problem H — Sample-size planning

If expected SD is 12 units and the desired 95% margin of error is 3 units, estimate the large-population sample size before and after allowing 10% loss.

Check: n₀ = (1.96 × 12 / 3)² ≈ 61.47, so round up to 62; allowing 10% loss gives 69.

🧠 Key Mnemonics

    

      

        Mean, Median, Mode
        
"MMM – Measure of Middle & Most"
Mean = average (sum/n), Median = middle value, Mode = most frequent.

      

      

        Standard Deviation
        
"Spread of Data – Square Root of Variance"
Remember: SD is in the same units as your data (fish length, weight, etc.).

      

      

        t-test
        
"Two groups? Think t!"
Paired for before-after on same individuals; independent for two separate groups.

      

      

        Chi-square
        
"χ² – Count the Difference"
Goodness-of-fit or Independence. Always check expected frequencies ≥ 5.

      

      

        Correlation vs Causation
        
"r is not cause!"
Pearson r measures linear association only. Biology needs experimental design for causality.

      

      

        ANOVA
        
"Analysis of Variance – More than two means"
Significant F means at least one difference; follow with post-hoc if needed.

📋 Formula Cheat-Sheet (Quick Reference)

    

      

        Descriptive Statistics
        
Mean: x̄ = Σx / n

        Median: Middle value (or average of two middle)

        Mode: Most frequent value

        Variance (sample): s² = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n-1)

        SD: s = √s²

      

      

        t-test (independent)
        
t = (x̄₁ - x̄₂) / √(s_p² (1/n₁ + 1/n₂))

        where s_p² = pooled variance

        Paired t: t = d̄ / (s_d / √n)

        df = n-1 for paired

      

      

        Chi-square
        
χ² = Σ (O - E)² / E

        Goodness-of-fit: df = k-1

        Independence: df = (r-1)(c-1)

        Expected E = (row total × column total) / grand total

      

      

        Correlation & Regression
        
Pearson r: r = Σ(x-x̄)(y-ȳ) / √[Σ(x-x̄)² Σ(y-ȳ)²]

        Regression: y = a + bx

        b = Σ(x-x̄)(y-ȳ) / Σ(x-x̄)²

        a = ȳ - b x̄

      

      

        One-way ANOVA
        
F = MS_between / MS_within

        MS_between = SS_between / (k-1)

        MS_within = SS_within / (N-k)

        Significant F → at least one mean differs

      

      

        Sample Size (approx)
        
For mean: n ≈ (Z · σ / E)²

        For proportion: n ≈ (Z² · p(1-p)) / E²

        Add 10-15% for non-response

      

    

    
Tip: Screenshot this card or note the formulas before exams. Always write the formula first, then substitute numbers.

  


  

    
📝 Worked Examples with Step-by-Step Solutions

    
    

      Example 1: Mean, Median, SD (Simpler language)
      
Problem: Fish lengths (cm): 12, 14, 15, 15, 18, 20

      Find mean, median and sample standard deviation.

      
Step-by-step:

      1. Mean = (12+14+15+15+18+20) / 6 = 94 / 6 = 15.67 cm

      2. Ordered data: 12, 14, 15, 15, 18, 20 → Median = average of 3rd & 4th = (15+15)/2 = 15 cm

      3. Deviations from mean: -3.67, -1.67, -0.67, -0.67, 2.33, 4.33

      4. Squared deviations sum ≈ 42.67

      5. Sample variance = 42.67 / 5 = 8.53

      6. SD = √8.53 ≈ 2.92 cm

      
Biological meaning: The average fish is about 15.7 cm long, and most fish are within roughly 3 cm of that average.

    


    

      Example 2: Paired t-test (Before vs After)
      
Problem: Same 5 fish weighed before and after a diet (grams).

      Before: 45, 50, 48, 52, 47 | After: 50, 55, 52, 58, 51

      
Step-by-step:

      1. Differences (After - Before): 5, 5, 4, 6, 4

      2. Mean difference d̄ = 24/5 = 4.8 g

      3. SD of differences s_d ≈ 0.84

      4. t = 4.8 / (0.84 / √5) = 4.8 / 0.376 ≈ 12.77

      5. df = 4. Critical t (0.05) ≈ 2.78. Since 12.77 > 2.78, significant increase.

      
Conclusion: The diet caused a statistically significant weight gain in these fish.

    


    

      Example 3: Chi-square Independence (Simpler)
      
Problem: Do two habitats differ in fish survival?

      Habitat A: 40 alive, 10 dead | Habitat B: 25 alive, 25 dead

      
Step-by-step:

      1. Totals: A=50, B=50, Alive=65, Dead=35, Grand=100

      2. Expected for A-Alive = (50×65)/100 = 32.5

      3. Calculate (O-E)²/E for all four cells → χ² ≈ 9.62

      4. df = 1. Critical value 3.84. 9.62 > 3.84 → significant association.

      
Biology: Survival depends on habitat. Habitat A is better for these fish.

    

  


  

    
🇮🇳 Tamil Support – Key Concepts (விரிவாக்கம்)

    

      

        Mean (சராசரி)
        
சராசரி = அனைத்து மதிப்புகளின் கூட்டுத்தொகை / எண்ணிக்கை

        உயிரியல்: மீன்களின் சராசரி நீளம் கணக்கிட பயன்படும்.

      

      

        Standard Deviation (திட்ட விலகல்)
        
தரவுகள் எவ்வளவு பரவியுள்ளன என்பதை காட்டுகிறது.

        சிறிய SD → தரவுகள் ஒன்றாக உள்ளன. பெரிய SD → அதிக வேறுபாடு.

      

      

        t-test
        
இரண்டு குழுக்களின் சராசரிகளுக்கு இடையே வேறுபாடு உள்ளதா என்று பார்க்க.

        ஒரே விலங்குகளை இரண்டு முறை அளந்தால் → Paired t-test.

      

      

        Chi-square (காய்-சதுரம்)
        
எதிர்பார்ப்பு எண்ணிக்கைக்கும் உண்மையான எண்ணிக்கைக்கும் இடையே வேறுபாடு உள்ளதா என்று பார்க்க.

        உயிரினங்களின் எண்ணிக்கை அல்லது வாழ்வு விகிதம் ஆய்வு செய்ய பயன்படும்.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

    
    
      
  • Using independent t-test for paired data (same animals measured twice).
    • 
      
  • Forgetting to check assumptions (normality, equal variance).
    • 
      
  • Reporting p-value without effect size or biological context.
    • 
      
  • Confusing population SD with sample SD (use n-1 for sample).
    • 
      
  • Calculating percentage change incorrectly on small numbers.
    • 
      
  • Ignoring expected frequency rules in chi-square.
